# Visual Direction

Use this when the visual world is new, unclear, or being replaced.

## Ground the direction in the subject

Start from the product itself, not from a dashboard category.

Look for:

- audience and usage scene
- physical or cultural materials associated with the subject
- vocabulary and domain artifacts
- tempo: calm, urgent, playful, ceremonial, technical, domestic, editorial
- content type: numbers, media, text, maps, timelines, creative work
- expected session length
- interaction frequency
- trust level and consequence of mistakes

A logistics console and a music discovery app should not arrive at the same visual language merely because both are web apps.

## Surface mode

Use the specific surface, not the company category.

### Workflow
Repeated task completion. Favor scanability, stable placement, clear states, efficient density, and quiet confidence.

### Reading
Comprehension. Favor measure, hierarchy, navigation, annotation, and typography.

### Persuasion
Decision and action. Favor narrative, proof, contrast, and one strong action path.

### Immersion
The content or artifact is the experience. Interface recedes; composition and transitions may carry more personality.

### Browse / Collect
Discovery, comparison, saving, returning, fandom, shopping, media, and collections. Favor identity, browsing rhythm, visible relationships, previews, and satisfying movement between items without sacrificing findability.

## Create alternatives on open axes

When the brief leaves major visual axes open, consider at least two materially different directions.

For each candidate, specify:

- 4–6 named palette roles, not just "blue" or "dark"
- type direction for display/body/utility roles when relevant
- composition concept
- surface/material strategy
- interaction character
- one signature element or memorable behavior

When recent unrelated outputs already share a strong material world, at least one candidate must break from that world at the material/typographic level, not merely by changing accent color.

Do not render ten random themes.

If one axis is the uncertainty, vary that axis first:

- palette
- type
- density
- layout structure
- emphasis
- motion vocabulary

Changing every axis at once teaches you nothing about why a direction works.

## Detect model-default convergence

Treat recurring material worlds as suspicious when the subject does not justify them.

Examples include:

- soft SaaS cards with muted blue
- dark neon technical surfaces
- glass/glow "premium" shells
- monochrome terminals with one status accent
- editorial-paper / archival-instrument surfaces built from warm off-white, dark ink, thin rules, and restrained earthy accents
- broadsheet-like zero-radius compositions used mainly as an anti-SaaS move

They are not banned. Repetition across unrelated products is the warning.

Do not escape one category rut by repeatedly choosing the same anti-default rut.

## Second-order convergence check

Before locking the visual direction, describe it without product nouns in one short material sentence.

Example shape:

> warm paper field + dark ink + thin rules + archival typography + restrained signal color

If essentially the same sentence could describe several unrelated recent outputs, the direction is probably converging even when the layouts differ.

When recent unrelated work is available, deliberately explore at least one candidate with a materially different:

- surface/material metaphor
- color temperature and contrast structure
- typography voice
- line/elevation language
- image/texture strategy

Do not merely recolor the same material world.

The goal is not forced novelty. It is to make the product earn its visual world from its own subject, audience, and task.

## Signature, not decoration

A signature is one memorable aspect that expresses the product.

Examples:

- a route timeline that doubles as navigation
- a morphing selected state that preserves spatial context
- a distinctive typographic index
- a comparison surface unique to the domain
- a media interaction grounded in the subject

Do not spread novelty across every component.

One authored idea surrounded by disciplined UI is usually stronger than many unrelated effects.

## Aesthetic risk

When the user grants visual freedom, make at least one non-default choice you can justify.

It may be:

- composition
- type pairing
- density
- surface treatment
- motion
- color
- navigation model

Risk does not mean spectacle. A severe, quiet layout can be the risk if the category usually defaults to cards.

## Direction lock

Before detailed implementation, ensure:

- the primary job is visible in the composition
- the palette is not a model habit
- typography supports the intended tone
- the memorable element serves the product
- the direction can survive desktop and mobile
- the direction does not depend on filler copy

Then build consistently from that direction.


## Name the category rut before exploring

Before choosing directions, name:

- the category's most predictable interface
- the obvious anti-default people use to avoid it

Example:

- default: soft SaaS cards
- anti-default: zero-radius broadsheet

Do not let all candidate directions come from either pole.

Look beyond web UI into the audience's real visual culture:

- physical tools
- packaging
- posters
- notation
- magazines
- catalogs
- venue graphics
- maps
- tickets
- product labels
- game/media interfaces
- archival systems
- industrial markings
- community/fandom artifacts

The goal is not costume.

Use these sources to discover structure, type, materials, rhythm, and interaction that belong to the subject.
